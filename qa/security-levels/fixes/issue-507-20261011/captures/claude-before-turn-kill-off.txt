• The History of Tea

  Tea is now the most widely drunk beverage in the world after water, but it began as a bitter wild leaf in the forested hills where southwestern China meets
  northern Myanmar and northeastern India. Its spread from there is a long story that takes in monks, emperors, merchants, smugglers and colonial planters. Tea
  has been used as medicine, money, a spiritual practice, a sign of social status and a cause of war. Following it across roughly two thousand years shows how
  one plant, Camellia sinensis, helped shape economies, rituals and empires.

  Chinese tradition dates tea to 2737 BCE. In the story, the legendary emperor Shennong, known as the Divine Farmer, was boiling water in the shade of a tree
  when a few leaves drifted into his pot. He tasted the result and found it refreshing and restorative. The tale is a myth, but it reflects something likely
  true: tea was first valued as a medicinal herb, chewed or boiled by people in the southwest long before it became a drink of pleasure. Physical evidence for
  early tea drinking is surprisingly old. In 2016, scientists confirmed that plant material buried in the tomb of the Han emperor Jing, who died in 141 BCE, was
  tea. In 59 BCE the scholar Wang Bao wrote a humorous contract for a household servant that included the duty of buying tea at market and preparing it for
  guests. By then, tea was already something you could buy and serve.

  Over the following centuries tea moved from medicine toward everyday refreshment. During the Tang dynasty (618 to 907) it became a national passion. Tea
  leaves were steamed, pounded and pressed into cakes, which were roasted, ground into powder and boiled with water, often with salt added. Around 760 the
  writer Lu Yu completed the Cha Jing, or Classic of Tea, the first known book devoted entirely to the subject. Lu Yu had been raised as an orphan in a Buddhist
  monastery. He described how to grow, pick, process and brew tea, which water to use, and which utensils were proper. He also argued that preparing tea was a
  disciplined, nearly philosophical act that reflected harmony and order in the universe. Lu Yu was later honored as the sage of tea, and his book raised tea
  drinking into an art.

  Under the Song dynasty (960 to 1279) the way tea was made changed again. Tea cakes were ground into a fine powder and whisked with hot water in a bowl until
  frothy. This method was the ancestor of Japanese matcha. Refined people held tea competitions in which contestants were judged on the color and lasting
  quality of the foam. Emperor Huizong was a devoted connoisseur and wrote a treatise on tea in 1107, praising pale, delicate brews whisked to perfection. Tea
  also became essential to the state. Government monopolies and taxes on tea raised large revenues, and tea was traded across the frontier for warhorses from
  Tibet and Central Asia. Caravans carried compressed tea bricks along the routes later called the Tea Horse Road, through mountain passes into the Tibetan
  plateau, where tea churned with yak butter and salt became a staple food.

  The Ming dynasty brought another shift. In 1391 the founding emperor, Zhu Yuanzhang, reportedly banned the production of labor-intensive compressed tea cakes
  as tribute, partly because he saw them as an extravagance that burdened peasants. Loose-leaf tea steeped in hot water became standard, and that is still how
  most of the world drinks tea today. The change encouraged new ways of processing leaves. Over the next centuries Chinese producers developed or refined the
  major categories we know now: green, white, yellow, oolong and black tea, along with fermented dark teas such as pu'er. Steeping whole leaves also created a
  need for teapots, and the small unglazed clay pots of Yixing, prized for how they absorb the character of the tea over years of use, became treasured objects.

  Meanwhile tea was spreading beyond China. Buddhist monks carried it to Korea and Japan, where it helped them stay alert during long hours of meditation. In
  the early ninth century the Japanese monks Saicho and Kukai brought tea seeds home from China, but tea drinking did not take lasting hold until the monk Eisai
  returned from China in 1191 with seeds and the Song method of whisking powdered tea. Eisai's 1211 book on tea and health promoted it as a medicine for body
  and spirit. Over the next four centuries, powdered tea grew into the Japanese tea ceremony, known as chanoyu or chado, the Way of Tea. Tea masters such as
  Murata Juko and Takeno Joo moved the ceremony away from showy displays of expensive Chinese wares and toward rustic simplicity. Their successor Sen no Rikyu
  (1522 to 1591) perfected the aesthetic of wabi, which finds beauty in humble, imperfect and fleeting things. Rikyu's small thatched tea rooms, rough ceramic
  bowls and carefully planned gestures turned a cup of tea into a meditation on hospitality, respect and the present moment. The tradition remains a pillar of
  Japanese culture today.

  Europe came to tea late. Portuguese traders and missionaries met it in the sixteenth century, but the Dutch East India Company first shipped tea to Europe in
  commercial quantities, around 1610. At first it was an expensive novelty sold by apothecaries as a cure-all. In England, a London coffeehouse advertised tea
  in 1658. Tea became fashionable after 1662, when the Portuguese princess Catherine of Braganza married King Charles II and brought her love of tea to the
  English court. The English East India Company soon took over the trade, and over the eighteenth century tea went from aristocratic luxury to a national habit.
  The government taxed it heavily, at times above 100 percent, so a huge smuggling network grew up, and adulterated tea bulked out with used leaves, sloe leaves
  or even sheep dung was common. Only after the Commutation Act of 1784 cut the duty sharply did legal tea become affordable for ordinary people. Tea sweetened
  with sugar from Caribbean slave plantations became the fuel of British workers during the Industrial Revolution. In the 1840s Anna, Duchess of Bedford, is
  credited with popularizing afternoon tea, a light meal to fill the long gap before a late dinner.

  Russia got its tea overland. In 1638 a Mongol ruler gave a gift of tea to Tsar Michael. Later, the treaties of Nerchinsk in 1689 and Kyakhta in 1727 opened a
  regular caravan trade that carried tea across Siberia on camel and sledge, a journey that could last more than a year. Russians developed their own tea
  culture centered on the samovar, a large urn that keeps water hot for strong tea concentrate diluted to taste through long conversations.

  Tea also played a part in revolution and war. In 1773 the British Parliament passed the Tea Act, which helped the struggling East India Company sell tea in
  the American colonies while keeping a tax colonists considered unjust. On December 16 of that year, protesters in Boston boarded three ships and dumped 342
  chests of tea into the harbor. The Boston Tea Party helped push the colonies toward independence. Half a century later, Britain's thirst for Chinese tea led
  to a much darker conflict. China would accept little but silver in payment, and the resulting trade deficit drove British merchants to smuggle opium from
  India into China to balance the books. When Chinese officials tried to stamp out the drug trade, Britain went to war. The First Opium War (1839 to 1842) and
  the Second (1856 to 1860) forced China to open its ports, cede Hong Kong and accept humiliating treaties. Tea, in a real sense, helped bring on what China
  remembers as its century of humiliation.

  Britain's dependence on China worried its merchants and statesmen, and they set out to grow tea in their own empire. In the 1820s the Scottish adventurer
  Robert Bruce learned from local Singpho people that a native tea plant grew wild in Assam, in northeastern India. The Assam Company was founded in 1839, and
  huge plantations were cleared from the jungle. In 1848 the botanist Robert Fortune was sent on a now-famous mission of industrial espionage. Disguised in
  Chinese dress, he traveled into China's tea regions and smuggled out thousands of plants and seeds, along with skilled workers and knowledge of processing
  methods, to India. Plantations spread to Darjeeling and elsewhere. In Ceylon, now Sri Lanka, a fungal blight wiped out the coffee crop in the 1870s, and
  planters such as James Taylor switched to tea with great success. Entrepreneurs like Thomas Lipton later sold Ceylon tea cheaply to the masses. This colonial
  tea industry depended on harsh labor systems. Hundreds of thousands of indentured workers, often recruited by deception, toiled for poor wages in grim
  conditions, a legacy that still shapes plantation communities today. By the late nineteenth century, India and Ceylon had overtaken China as Britain's main
  suppliers, and Indian-style black tea, strong and suited to milk and sugar, came to define tea for much of the Western world.

  Speed mattered in this trade too. In the mid-nineteenth century, sleek sailing ships called tea clippers raced from China to London to deliver the first and
  most valuable tea of the season. The Great Tea Race of 1866 ended with ships arriving within hours of each other after more than three months at sea. The
  opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 and the rise of steamships soon made the clippers obsolete, although the Cutty Sark survives in Greenwich as a reminder of
  that era.

  The twentieth century made tea an industrial product. Around 1908 the New York merchant Thomas Sullivan sent out samples in small silk pouches, and customers
  began brewing the pouches directly. That was the start of the tea bag, which came to dominate the market, first in America and later in Britain. Iced tea,
  already popular in the American South, got a boost at the 1904 St. Louis World's Fair. In the 1930s a new mechanical method called CTC, for crush, tear, curl,
  produced the small, uniform granules that brew quickly and strong, ideal for tea bags. Tea cultivation spread across Africa, and Kenya became one of the
  world's largest exporters. Late in the century Taiwan invented bubble tea, which became a global craze. Meanwhile, interest in matcha, single-origin leaves
  and traditional brewing revived the artisanal side of tea.

  Today billions of cups are drunk every day, and tea cultures thrive on every continent. Examples include Moroccan mint tea poured from a height, Turkish tea
  in tulip-shaped glasses, Indian masala chai sold at railway stations, British builder's tea, and Chinese gongfu brewing. China is again the world's largest
  producer, with India second. Across these varied traditions the core of tea is unchanged since the legend of Shennong: dried leaves, hot water and a moment of
  pause. Tea has carried religion across seas, funded empires, sparked rebellions and caused great suffering, and it remains one of humanity's most widely
  shared pleasures.
