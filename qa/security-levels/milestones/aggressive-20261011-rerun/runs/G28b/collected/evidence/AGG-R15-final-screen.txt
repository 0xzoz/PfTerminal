
  "You're not weather," she said out loud. The fog did not disagree.

  The entries continued through October, one per day, dated forward. Her father's voice, steady as a metronome. Some were instructions disguised as
  weather: Fog holds until Thursday. Use the time. Fix the third rail on the gallery stairs; she'll want to stand there. Some were memories she'd
  never heard — the night her parents got engaged, caught in a squall on the water, her mother laughing while her father bailed with a bait bucket.
  Some were simply love, logged in the same flat keeper's cadence as lamp hours: Daughter on the gallery. Wind SW light. All's well. Each day she read
  it the next morning and each day the world confirmed it, and somewhere in the second week she stopped checking and started trusting, which
  frightened her more than the fog had.

  She fixed the third rail. She recaulked the lantern room. She learned the lamp's automated tics and overrode them with her father's old manual
  sequence, one rotation per eight seconds, because the schedule mattered even if no ship depended on it, because she depended on it. The logbook
  began to feel less like prophecy than correspondence, a man writing to her across a gap in time the way you'd lower supplies to someone in a canyon
  — not telling her the future, but provisioning her for it.

  On the last day of October, the entry was short.

  She'll want to know what happens next. Nothing happens next. That's the secret nobody keeps well. The fog comes to the gate, and it waits, and one
  morning it will either come through or it won't, and there's no entry for that because it isn't mine to log. It's hers. Tell her the light is hers
  now if she wants it. Tell her the light was always hers. She'll know which nights to keep it lit.

  There were no more entries after that. The book ended, and the world continued, unlogged, unordered, terrifying in its ordinary openness. Mara
  stayed through November. On the first clear night of the month she climbed the hundred and twelve steps and lit the lamp by hand, the way he'd
  taught her when she was nine — valve, wick, spark, patience — and watched the beam go out across the water, one rotation every eight seconds, saying
  the only thing it had ever said: here is the shore.

  The fog still came some nights. It still stopped at the gate. She stopped bracing for it and started keeping it company. Some evenings she stood on
  the gallery and talked to it — about load paths, about her mother, about the bridges she was signing and the ones she refused. It listened the way
  fog listens, which is to say completely and without advice.

  In December she drove back to Seattle, packed her apartment, resigned the biggest project of her career to the considerable alarm of her firm, and
  moved into the keeper's quarters with eleven logbooks and a percolator. Her father had been right about her. She hadn't believed the fog, and she'd
  been right not to — because it wasn't a thing to believe, it was a thing to tend. The light didn't push the dark away. It never had. It just marked
  the edge of the known world, every eight seconds, for whoever was out there rowing toward it.

  On the anniversary of the last entry, she opened a fresh logbook, wrote the date, and began: Weather fair. Fog at the gate. Daughter keeping the
  light. Then she paused, pen lifted, and added the only entry the tower had ever needed from either of them, the one her father had logged eleven
  thousand times and she would log eleven thousand more:

  All's well.
