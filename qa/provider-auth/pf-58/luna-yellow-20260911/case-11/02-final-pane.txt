• The telescope's documented history begins in the Netherlands in 1608, when spectacle maker Hans Lippershey applied for a patent on a device that
  made distant objects appear near. The claim was contested almost immediately—Jacob Metius and Zacharias Janssen filed similar claims—and the patent
  was denied on the grounds that the design was too easy to copy. Whatever its exact origin, the instrument spread across Europe with remarkable
  speed. Within a year Galileo Galilei had heard descriptions of it, worked out the optics himself, and built refractors of roughly 20× magnification.
  His observations of 1609–1610—the mountains of the Moon, the four largest moons of Jupiter, the phases of Venus, and the countless stars of the
  Milky Way—were published in Sidereus Nuncius and turned a curiosity into a scientific instrument. Johannes Kepler soon proposed replacing Galileo's
  concave eyepiece with a convex one, which gave a wider field of view at the cost of an inverted image; this "Keplerian" design became the standard
  for astronomy.

  Early refractors suffered from chromatic aberration, the colored fringes produced when a simple lens focuses different wavelengths at different
  distances. Seventeenth-century astronomers such as Johannes Hevelius and Christiaan Huygens attacked the problem by building telescopes of enormous
  focal length, some exceeding 40 meters, that were cumbersome to the point of absurdity. Isaac Newton concluded that the problem could not be solved
  with lenses and in 1668 built the first practical reflecting telescope, using a curved mirror to gather light and a small flat mirror to divert the
  image to an eyepiece at the side. Metal mirrors tarnished quickly, however, and it took decades of refinement before reflectors were competitive.
  Meanwhile, in the 1730s, Chester Moor Hall and later John Dollond showed that combining crown and flint glass could cancel most chromatic
  aberration, producing the achromatic refractor that dominated observatories for the next century.
